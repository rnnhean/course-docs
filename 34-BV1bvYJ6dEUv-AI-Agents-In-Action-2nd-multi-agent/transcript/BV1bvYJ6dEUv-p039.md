Section 7.3.4 Understanding the role of rubrics in evaluation The evaluation strategy depends on what the agent is producing. Agents that produce structured outputs against a known target, classification labels, extracted fields, optimization decisions, can be evaluated using traditional metrics such as accuracy,
[07:37] precision, recall, and F1 score. The right or wrong question has a clean answer in these cases, and the standard ML evaluation toolkit applies directly. Agents that produce open-ended natural language outputs, summaries, explanations, plans, creative content, require different methods. Accuracy is rarely well-defined when multiple phrasings can be correct, and quality is multi-dimensional, which is why rubrics, LLM as judge evaluators,
[08:06] and Human Review take over from numerical metrics. Most production agents are a mix of both kinds, with structured pieces evaluated numerically and natural language pieces evaluated through rubrics or by judges. Picking the right evaluation method for each piece of the agent's output is part of the design work. Defaulting to rubrics when accuracy would suffice creates unnecessary evaluation complexity and defaulting to accuracy when rubrics are needed
[08:34] yields brittle measures that miss what actually matters. In education, a rubric defines structured criteria and standards a student must meet to receive a particular grade. The same idea applies to evaluating agent output with one important addition. The rubric itself has to be validated against human judgment or it scores consistently but consistently wrong. We can follow these steps to define a rubric to evaluate the output's performance. 1.
[09:02] Identify the purpose and objectives. Determine the goals you want the output to accomplish. For example, do you want to evaluate the quality of recommendations for a given audience or the overall quality for a given subject, format, or other input? 2. Define criteria. Develop a set of criteria or dimensions that you will use to evaluate the output. These criteria should align with your objectives and provide clear guidelines for assessment.
[09:29] Each criterion should be specific and measurable. For example, you may want to measure a recommendation by how well it fits the genre, subject, and format. 3. Create a scale. Establish a rating scale that describes the levels of performance for each criterion. Standard scales include numerical scales, for example, 1.5, or descriptive scales, for example, excellent, good, fair, poor. 4. Provide descriptions.
[09:58] For each level on the scale, provide clear and concise descriptions that indicate what constitutes a strong performance and what represents a weaker performance for each criterion. 5. Apply the rubric. When assessing a prompt or agent persona, use the rubric to evaluate the prompt's performance against the established criteria. Assign scores or ratings for each criterion based on the descriptions for each level. 6. Calculate the total score. Depending on your rubric,
[10:27] You may calculate a total score by summing up the scores for each criterion or using a weighted average if some criteria are more important than others. 7. Ensure evaluation consistency. If multiple evaluators are assessing the profile, ensure consistency in grading. 8. Review, revise, and iterate. Periodically review and revise the rubric to ensure it aligns with your assessment goals and objectives, adjusted as needed to improve its effectiveness.
